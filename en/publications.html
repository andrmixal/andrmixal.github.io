I am a patient with NIVL, non-thrombotic compression of the left common iliac vein, diagnosis I87.1. For almost thirty years my visible problem was a recurrent left varicocele. Recently I found my notes from a consultation with Professor Kostev, chief urologist of City Hospital No. 10 in Odesa. He explained the mechanism clearly, and I did not understand it then.

The notes record the main point. The notes call it a pseudo-pincer: the vein is clamped at the scar as between the jaws of pincers, a functional narrowing that is easy to miss. It is latent, with no leg edema. Removing the pseudo-pincer would resolve the symptoms: either with a stent from the inside, or by removing the external factor during a microsurgical testicular revascularization with the inferior epigastric vein.

This is a vicious circle. Narrowing of the iliac vein raises pressure in the gonadal veins, and varicocele appears as a symptom. The operation ligates the gonadal vein, a possible outflow path, and leaves a scar. The scar forms a pseudo-pincer, the pressure finds new routes, and the varicocele returns. The disease looks resistant to treatment, while the treatment itself has become part of its cause.

For twelve years a leading Odesa vascular surgeon, Smirnov, called the compression on imaging an artifact.

THE POKER FACE OF UKRAINE'S MINISTRY OF HEALTH

The photo shows a real venous stent. It is large, and that is exactly why its size matters.

This stent is made of nitinol, an alloy of nickel and titanium. During placement it is compressed inside a thin catheter, and inside the vessel it expands on its own to its preset shape. Nitinol is highly elastic, well tolerated by the body, corrosion resistant and flexible, so the stent can move together with the vein. A balloon is used before and after placement to open the narrowing and press the stent firmly against the vessel wall.

Iliac vein stenting for May-Thurner syndrome and similar compressions helps many urological patients. But even the best stent works well only when its size matches the vessel of that particular patient.

A vein is not a round pipe. Under compression it is flattened. Standard venography often underestimates this: in studies it missed a share of significant narrowings and underestimated vessel diameter. IVUS, intravascular ultrasound, shows the vein from the inside and gives its diameter, area and the length of the compressed segment. That is why IVUS is considered the gold standard for stent sizing worldwide.

A sizing error is not a detail. If the stent is too small, the risk of thrombosis and stent migration grows, and the pain may not go away. If it is too large, it can injure the vein wall or promontorium. For a vessel with a diameter of 16 mm, a difference of one millimeter is about 12 percent of the lumen area. Fixing this afterwards is very difficult.

Without IVUS, stent sizing is a lottery, not science. The doctor plays a game of chance and keeps a poker face, and the stake in that game is the patient's health. To my knowledge, in Ukraine iliac vein stents are usually placed without IVUS, using angiography only, because there is nothing to measure with.

That is why hospitals need IVUS equipment, so these procedures are safe and truly beneficial. Patients have the right to a precisely chosen stent.

Top Odesa surgeons, venous stents exist, and they are on sale in Ukraine. IVUS catheters are not, yet. Here is what that means in practice.

R.E. Mamedov has stented veins since 2013. His message in one line: do it right the first time, because fixing it later is miserable.

Why urologists and gynecologists should care: non-thrombotic iliac vein compression (NIVL) affects men and women equally. It can show up as varicocele or gynecological complaints.

What matters:

1. Build a team: vascular or endovascular surgeon, urologist/gynecologist, imaging, anesthesia.

2. Equipment: angiography suite over a C-arm, IV sedation with monitoring (deployment and dilation hurt), expert class ultrasound.

3. Instruments: full range of stents and balloons, long stiff wires, 10 to 11 French introducers. One stent and one wire per patient, with no spare, is not right. If you do not have Ivus then you must at least have a proctor present.

4. Stent choice: for obstructions, beginners should avoid old good Wallstent. Its radial force is weak at the ends and it shortens when stretched. Choose latest generation dedicated venous stents.

5. Sizing: IVUS helps, but without it, size by CT venography, generally 14 mm and up. Without IVUS the operator needs real experience in choosing size and position. Oversized means long lasting back pain. Choose access by compression level, balloon the stented segment well, and overlap multiple stents correctly.

6. Afterwards: LMWH in hospital, then optional prophylactic anticoagulant for up to six months. Cheap old stents can thrombose, fold and migrate, so if symptoms return, even years later, investigate.

Top causes of failure: wrong length or diameter estimate, mismatched stent and balloon, no pre-dilation, bad overlap, arterial stents in veins.

Bottom line: re-stenting is far harder than doing it right once. No experience or instruments? Invite a proctor who does this routinely. Patients do not thank you for a creative learning curve.

Negative hallucination in urology, or how to miss the elephant in the room

A positive hallucination is seeing what is not there. A negative one is not seeing what is there. Dr. A. A. Kapto, Doctor of Medical Sciences, said in a lecture that many urologists are in the second state when it comes to venous causes of pelvic pain. Let us test it on varicocele.

The typical story. The left testicular vein is dilated, the patient has pain, the diagnosis is ready: varicocele. The question "why did it dilate?" sounds like a provocation in many offices.

The answer often lies higher up. An artery presses the left iliac vein against the spine (May-Thurner syndrome). Blood looks for a detour and flows backward through the testicular vein. The vein dilates not because it is a bad vein, but because of pressure.

According to Dr. Kapto, among his examined patients with pelvic venous disorders, primary forms were only 15.5 percent, and secondary ones, with an anatomical cause, were 84.5 percent. So most of the time something is pressing on the vein from outside.

Meanwhile, as the lecturer put it, the European Association of Urology guidelines on chronic pelvic pain, published since 2003 with the latest update in 2025, contain no vascular venous factor. More than twenty years of searching without a single match. This is not inattention, it is consistency.

Imagine a garden hose with someone standing on it. The water behind it swells the hose. No sane person would cut out the swollen piece and stitch it up. Everyone looks for who is standing on the hose. Yet varicocele is routinely treated as if the hose swelled by itself, for personal reasons.

Compression is treated by decompression. Everything else is fighting the consequence, while the cause stays calmly put and the doctor, fascinated by the little elephant, never once looks up to see where the pressure comes from.

Urologists, here is a simple cure for negative hallucination: before operating on varicocele, look at the anatomy. CT, venography, and the little elephant becomes invisible, while the compression becomes visible.

May-Thurner Syndrome: Four Compression Zones Ukrainian Doctors Somehow Never Heard Of

Turns out squeezing a vein isn't a one-trick pony. At Mount Sinai in New York, vascular surgeons casually discuss four different ways an artery can sit on an iliac vein like it owes it money. Meanwhile, plenty of local Ukrainian textbooks are still stuck on the "one classic spot" story, bless them.

The right common iliac artery crushes the left common iliac vein. The original, the classic, the one everyone's heard of, vascular medicine's greatest hit.

The right external iliac artery leans on the right common or external iliac vein. Plot twist: it doesn't have to be the left leg.

The right internal (hypogastric) iliac artery presses the right external iliac vein. Yes, arteries can gang up from more than one direction.

The left internal iliac artery compresses the left external iliac vein. Even the "safe" side isn't safe.

Without IVUS, good luck spotting any of this. Standard venography and regular ultrasound tend to flatten the vein into what specialists charmingly call the "pancake sign", technical term for "we can't see it, so it must be fine."

Result: dozens of years of recurrent varicocele, swelling, pain, recurring clots, and post-thrombotic syndrome, all treated with blood thinners that were never going to fix a mechanical roadblock, because pills don't unpinch a vein.

And the training senior State Institute fellows keep churning out one-zone thinking, so the other three compression types stay comfortably invisible.

Modern standards, updated curricula, and actual IVUS equipment in the OR would help. Revolutionary idea, we know.

Iliac vein stenting: what the latest data (and expert warnings) mean for Ukraine

Dr. Brooke Spencer (interventional radiologist, USA) just published new data in eClinicalMedicine (Lancet family journal) that should matter to every vascular specialist and patient advocate in Ukraine.

The numbers:

- 271 women with POTS/orthostatic intolerance treated with iliac vein stenting

- At one year: 44% no longer met criteria for orthostatic intolerance at all, 71% showed statistically significant improvement

- Pelvic pain, interstitial cystitis symptoms, and quality of life scores improved to the same degree

- 80-90% of these patients also had pelvic pain, GI symptoms, urinary urgency, syncope, a symptom pattern rarely connected to iliac vein compression in routine practice

- Only 55% had the "classic" bilateral leg symptoms; 13% left-sided only. Waiting for the textbook swollen left leg before considering this diagnosis is outdated.

The same underlying anatomy and pathology exist in men. The clinical marker there is most often recurrent varicocele, not pelvic pain. This is a population almost nobody is screening for iliac vein compression, because varicocele is still treated as a purely urological problem.

Here's the part worth sitting with: a 2015 meta-analysis of 1,122 non-thrombotic patients reported 96-99% one-year stent patency. That is a genuinely good number, since stents staying open is good. But Dr. Spencer's point is that patency alone is the wrong finish line. A stent can stay open and still be the wrong size, in the wrong position, or slowly causing thrombus, vena cava compromise, or migration, problems that don't show up in a simple "open vs. closed" statistic. For young patients who will live with these stents for 50-60 years, the real question isn't just "does it stay open," but "was it sized and placed correctly in the first place." That requires intravascular ultrasound (IVUS) and correct sizing based on Murray's law (the cube root of the vena cava radius should equal the sum of the cube roots of the two iliac vein radii, not simple oversizing).

Why this matters for Ukraine: IVUS-guided iliac vein stenting is not performed anywhere in the country, confirmed in writing by both the National Academy of Medical Sciences and the Shalimov Institute. This isn't a niche gap. It affects young and old men and women with disabling, unexplained symptoms, and men with recurrent varicocele alike, and closing it requires more than importing a stent. It requires the equipment and the technique to place it correctly.

The stenting procedure for May-Thurner Syndrome is a high-tech, minimally invasive surgery. It requires no incisions, only a small puncture.

Surgical Procedure

* Access: Under local anesthesia, the doctor makes a puncture in a vein. This is typically the popliteal vein (behind the knee) or the femoral vein.

* Insertion: A thin catheter is inserted through the puncture and guided to the site of the compression behind the pelvic bone.

* IVUS (Intravascular Ultrasound): A probe is inserted into the vein to perform an ultrasound from the inside. The surgeon sees the exact site of compression and measures the diameter to ensure the Abre or Venovo stent fits perfectly.

* Deployment: The stent is guided to the compression site in a compressed state and then expanded. It pushes the artery and bone apart, restoring the vein's patency.

* Completion: The catheter is removed, and a pressure bandage is applied to the puncture site. The entire procedure takes 40–60 minutes.

Rehabilitation

First 24 Hours:

You will remain in the hospital. Bed rest is required to allow the puncture site to heal. You may feel discomfort or a "stretching" sensation in the pelvic area—this is normal as the vein has been expanded.

First Month:

* Blood Thinning: This is the most critical part. You will be prescribed anticoagulants (e.g., Xarelto or Eliquis). This is vital to prevent blood clots from forming on the new stent before it is fully integrated.

* Movement: Prolonged sitting or standing is prohibited. You must walk for 10–15 minutes every couple of hours. Walking helps "pump" blood through the stent.

* Restrictions: Lifting weights over 5 kg and deep squats are prohibited to avoid displacing the stent while it becomes embedded in the vein wall (endothelialization).

When to Expect Relief

The burning pain and pressure behind the pelvic bone often disappear immediately after surgery or within the first few days. The heaviness in the testicle (varicocele) decreases gradually over 1–3 months, as blood stops refluxing downward and begins to exit through the stent.
